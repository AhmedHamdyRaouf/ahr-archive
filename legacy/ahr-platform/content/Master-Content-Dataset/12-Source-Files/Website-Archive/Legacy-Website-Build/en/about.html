Origins & Roots

From Maharam Bek Quarter
to the Heart of Arab Art

Born on August 15, 1984, in the Maharam Bek quarter of Alexandria. His home was no ordinary home — it was a living studio buzzing with music, theatre, and rehearsals, where his father, the great composer Hamdy Raouf, filled the walls with melodies and the sound of instruments.

In third grade, he came across a poetry collection by poet Mohamed Ali Shabana. A text titled "Gratitude... A Word the Birds Knew" caught his eye. He sat alone and set it to music without any guidance. When his father heard the melody, he knew his son possessed an extraordinary natural gift.

In fourth grade — before the age of ten — he composed and arranged a complete children's musical theatre production of 14 songs and several musical pieces. That was the true beginning of a career that has never stopped since.

The First Root

Hamdy Raouf
1953 — 2013

The great theatre composer Hamdy Raouf was more than a father — he was the first teacher, the real school, and the legacy his son carries in every note he composes. Known for composing dozens of theatrical musicals that defined Egyptian theatre history, he was among the most prominent composers who preserved the spirit of the theatrical song in Egypt.

Ahmed grew up in a home where rehearsal was routine and art was discussed at the dinner table. He accompanied his father to training sessions, helped arrange his works, and absorbed what no book can teach — the instinct of a theatre composer, and the ability to listen to the word before the melody.

In 2013, Hamdy Raouf passed away, leaving a vast artistic legacy and a son dedicated to continuing the journey and honouring the name he carries with full integrity.

"Every melody I compose is a conversation with my father's soul."

Artistic Journey

A Quarter Century in Service
of Word and Melody

Before discussing the journey, one must understand a fundamental distinction often misunderstood — the difference between a Song Composer (Mulahhin) and a Music Composer (Mu'allif Musiqi).

The Song Composer (Mulahhin) is the source of music for words — in Arabic or any language. He pours the soul into the text, transforms letters into feelings, makes words soar or weep or celebrate. He reads the poetic text with a poet's eye and grasps its keys with a musician's heart.

The Music Composer (Mu'allif Musiqi) creates music and gives it meaning through instruments — without words. Each instrument carries a distinct character: the violin weeps and yearns, the oud narrates and contemplates, the piano oscillates between grandeur and lightness, rhythm moves the body before the mind. The true music composer plays upon this collective memory with the skill of a painter on a blank canvas.

Ahmed Hamdy Raouf combines both — among the rare few who master this union with integrity and depth.

01
Songs & Collaborations

The Song — Where Word Meets Soul

In the world of song, Ahmed Hamdy Raouf holds one uncompromising principle: the word comes first. Before placing a single note, he reads the text again and again — searching for its internal pulse, for the pivotal word that carries the poem's entire weight, for the dramatic moment that, when struck by melody, ignites the poem in the listener's heart.

He calls this "The Drama of the Word in the Melody" — his artistic philosophy that gives his compositions a fingerprint unlike any other. A song is not music accompanying words; it is words finding the authentic music that was waiting for them.

This is evident in his collaborations with Arab singing legends: Mohamed Mounir who found in "Ana Mennak Itelammt" a melody expressing a universal human experience — performed in Germany, Spain, and Egypt. Ali El-Haggar for whom he composed over forty songs, each an independent world — from the sincere patriotism of "Fagr Biladi" (winner of the Oscar for Best Music Video), to the veiled romance of "Oyounak Oyouni", the Upper Egyptian soul of "A El Rabab", and the profound humanity of "Tabtab Be Rohak". And Medhat Saleh who carried "Qalb Wahid" to the big screen in the film Room 707, then "Benehibbak Ya Drama" which became the official song of the Cairo Drama Festival 2022.

In every collaboration, Ahmed studies the singer's voice first — not just technically, but dramatically. He asks: what can this voice say that no other can? What emotional territory does this voice inhabit alone? Then he writes the melody precisely within that territory.

02
Drama & Cinema

Soundtrack — When Music Tells What Dialogue Cannot

Ahmed's relationship with soundtrack composition is not a profession he learned — it is an inheritance he lived inside. Since childhood, he sat beside his father in recording sessions, watching how a musical theme is built, how it evolves alongside the characters, and how silence itself is deployed as a musical instrument.

His participation began with musical preparation for his father's works, progressing to arrangement, then independent composition, finally arriving at what is known as the "Complete Musical Vision" — a concept beyond traditional soundtrack work encompassing: musical themes for each character, the emotional logic of each scene, and the complete sonic identity of the work from beginning to end.

This manifested across documentary films, each carrying an entirely different soul: In "Badia Masabni", the challenge was to conjure the spirit of an entire era — the artistic elegance of Cairo in its pioneering age — without falling into rigid imitation or shallow nostalgia. The music served as a time machine, returning the viewer to a world they never lived in, yet felt they were remembering. In "The Egyptian National Theatre", Ahmed built a sonic identity worthy of an institution whose history spans generations — music that carries the weight of legacy and the responsibility of documentation, while remaining alive and resonant for today's audience. In "Hekayet El Ferqa (The Troupe's Story)", the soundtrack operated on two layers simultaneously: one accompanying and supporting the narrative, and a deeper layer commenting on it in silence — as if saying what none of the film's protagonists could say themselves.

In soundtrack work, Ahmed believes that music does not accompany the image — it completes what the image cannot express. It reveals what is inside a character when they go silent, deepens the moment of silence when dialogue ceases, and gives rhythm to the visual edit.

03
Musical Geography

Music & Place — The Father's Deepest Lesson

Among the most precious things Hamdy Raouf passed on to his son was this profound lesson: Music is not one universal language; it is a collection of dialects, each place speaking from it in a different tongue.

Egypt alone — this single land — carries within it multiple musical worlds that bear no resemblance to one another:

  • Upper Egyptian Music (Al-Sa'id) — with its heavy rhythms and firm folkloric character, where the rababa and simsimiyya narrate tales of solid earth and authentic people.
  • Rural & Agricultural Music — with warm melodies mirroring the rhythm of simple life on the Nile banks, where the tune resembles wind through the fields.
  • Coastal Music — Alexandria and Mediterranean cities — with its open Mediterranean spirit blending Eastern maqam with elements of Greek and Italian music.
  • Canal Zone Music — Port Said, Ismailia, and Damietta — with a distinct personality forged by the golden generation of port, sea, and trade music.
  • Bedouin Music — Northern and Southern Bedouin — with entirely distinct maqamat and rhythms that speak of open space, nomadic life, and freedom.

What applies to Egypt applies to every country and region in the world. Each people carries a different musical ear shaped over centuries of history, culture, and environment. The composer who ignores these differences addresses his audience in a language their hearts cannot hear.

Ahmed studied this Musical Geography under his father's guidance with genuine passion, making it one of his deepest compositional tools. This is why "Dari Gamalak" differs in its Bedouin soul from "El Midan" with its urban energy, and both differ from "Qalb Wahid" with its cinematic metropolitan character.

04
Commercials & TV

The Music of Thirty Seconds — A Completely Different Art

Each domain of music production has its own distinct nature, radically different from the others — and this is what makes Ahmed Hamdy Raouf a rare model of someone who masters more than one domain with equal depth.

In commercial advertising, the game is entirely different. You don't have minutes to build atmosphere — you have seconds. The music must strike emotion instantly, carry the brand's identity, and remain lodged in memory after the ad ends. This demands precise psychological calibration of rhythm and a deep understanding of consumer psychology and auditory memory.

In television and programme music, the equation shifts again. Music here builds identity — it defines a programme, makes the viewer recognise the screen before seeing the image. This calls for a theme that is memorable and repeatable without fatigue, capable of enduring for years without growing stale.

In children's theatre and operettas, additional educational requirements emerge — music must simplify without becoming trivial, delight without complicating, carry values without moralising. This is among the most challenging and most collectively resonant of all that Ahmed has written.

"Real music is not only heard — it is felt. The true composer does not write music — he writes emotions that speak the language of music."

Voice Coaching Pioneer

One of the Top 7
Voice Coaches in the Arab World

Ahmed began his voice coaching journey early, participating in his father's sessions with singers and children. Over years of developing his own methodology in breathing, vocal dynamics, and advanced techniques, he became a trusted name among professional singers, actors, media personalities, and beginners alike.

His training relies on advanced techniques including breath control, articulation, resonance and vocal projection, and emotional dynamics in performance.

He has collaborated with Egypt's leading cultural and government institutions, overseeing the training of thousands of children and youth in major national projects.
